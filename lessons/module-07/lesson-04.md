# 07.4 · Knife and wood tools

A deep cut in the backcountry turns a routine night into an evacuation, and a femoral bleed can kill before help arrives. Most knife injuries are predictable: tired, cold, hurried, wrong knife, wrong position. Good habits cost nothing and let you do the useful work (kindling, feather sticks, pegs, pot hooks, repairs) all day without incident.

## Explanation

The knife is on every survival kit list (Stage 1). It is also the tool behind many bushcraft injuries: a slipped blade into the thigh, the hand holding the work, or a person who walked too close. Knife skill is mostly **geometry and habit**: where the edge can go if it slips, and where your body and other people are.

### The blood circle

Stretch your arm out with the knife in it and turn around. That circle is the **blood circle**. **No one else inside it while you cut; if someone steps in, you stop.** Carve seated, with your elbows on or outside your knees, so your arm's movement is braced and short.

### Where the edge goes if it slips

- **Cut away from your body**, and make sure the path of the stroke ends in empty air, not at your other hand, thigh or knee.
- **Never carve over your lap or between your knees.** The inner thigh carries the **femoral artery**, where a deep cut can bleed life-threateningly in minutes. Work **outside the knee**, or on a stump or log.
- Keep the **supporting hand behind the edge**, never in front of it.
- Use controlled, braced grips (the chest-lever grip and thumb-push cut are taught on bushcraft courses) that limit how far the blade can travel.

### Handling

- Sheath the knife when it is not in your hand. **Do not stick it in the ground** (grit dulls the edge, and someone steps on it) or leave it on a log.
- **Pass it handle first**, spine towards the receiver, and say "thank you" when you take it, so both people know who holds it.
- Never try to catch a falling knife. Step back.
- Carve in good light, rested, with warm hands. **Cold, tired, hurried** is when accidents happen. Stage 1's human-factors lesson applies directly.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Knife and tool law**
>
> Carrying knives in public is regulated everywhere, differently. In the UK, for example, only a non-locking folding knife with a cutting edge of 3 inches or less may be carried without a good reason. Many countries restrict fixed blades or locking knives in public places, schools, transport and parks. Carry the knife in your pack, not on your belt, when travelling to the trailhead. **Cutting live trees or saplings** needs the landowner’s permission and is prohibited in most parks. Practise on dead-fall, bought dowels, or wood from your own garden.

### Batoning: the knife as a wedge

Batoning means striking the spine of a knife with a wooden baton to split small rounds of wood. It reaches the dry core of wet wood (Stage 3) and makes kindling without an axe. It is safe only within limits:

- **Fixed blade, full tang**, sturdy spine. **Never a folding knife**: the lock or pivot fails, and the blade can close on your fingers.
- **Wrist-thick or smaller**, straight-grained, **knot-free** rounds. At a knot the grain changes direction and the blade jams or twists.
- The **tip must stick out** at least 5 cm beyond the round, so the baton can strike it. Strike the spine over the wood first, then the tip, keeping the blade level.
- Work on a **chopping block**, kneeling or standing to one side. Your other hand is never under the blade.

A saw and wooden wedges (**gluts**) are often safer and gentler on the knife. For fire-lighting, feather sticks from split dry wood are the goal.

### Wooden tools

Wood is a composite: long cellulose fibers bound in lignin. It is strong along the grain, weak across it, and tougher when it has some moisture than when it is bone-dry.

| Tool | How | The engineering |
|---|---|---|
| **Digging stick** | Wrist-thick hardwood, chisel point, dried near (not in) coals | A lever and a wedge. Drying the tip makes it harder. Charring it weakens it. |
| **Mallet / club** | Dense log section with a carved handle | Momentum ($p = mv$): a heavy head swung slowly delivers large, controlled blows without damaging a knife spine |
| **Wedges (gluts)** | Hardwood, tapered 1:6 to 1:10 | A shallower taper gives more splitting force per blow, but each blow advances it less |
| **Tent pegs** | Forked or notched sticks, 20–30 cm | Driven at about 90° to the guy line, or leaning slightly away from it. The notch holds the line. |
| **Pot hook / trammel** | Forked green branch with notches | Green wood resists fire; adjusting notches changes the pot height and so the heat |
| **Tongs** | A green stick bent double, or split and sprung | Spring steel's cheap cousin, for hot stones (Lesson 3) and coals |
| **Spoons, spatulas** | Carved from green straight-grained wood | Green wood carves easily and seasons without cracking if dried slowly |

**Improvised tools** from rubbish follow the same logic. A tin lid becomes a scraper, a bent wire a pot hook, a glass shard a cutting edge (with great care).

> [!WARNING]
> **If a cut happens**
>
> Carry a first-aid kit whenever you carve, and know how to use it. For serious bleeding: **firm direct pressure**; pack deep wounds; use a tourniquet for life-threatening limb bleeding (Stage 9). Take a hands-on WFA/WAFA course. Reading is not enough for bleeding control.

## Scientific and technical background

### Pressure: why sharp is safer

A blade cuts when the **pressure** under its edge exceeds what the material can resist:

$$
P = \frac{F}{A} = \frac{F}{L \cdot w}
$$

$F$ is your push (N), $L$ the length of edge in contact and $w$ the effective edge width.

**Worked example.** Push with 20 N along 10 mm of edge. A **sharp** edge about 1 µm wide gives $A = 10^{-2} \times 10^{-6} = 10^{-8}$ m², so $P = 2 \times 10^{9}$ Pa (2 GPa). A **dull** edge 50 µm wide needs **50× the force**, 1,000 N, to reach the same pressure. You cannot push that hard with control. Instead you push hard, the blade skates, and the stroke ends somewhere you did not plan. **Sharp knives cut with small, controllable forces.**

### The wedge

A wedge converts a strike along its length into a much larger force sideways. Ignoring friction, the mechanical advantage is about

$$
MA \approx \frac{h}{t}
$$

where $h$ is the height of the wedge face (blade height) and $t$ its thickness at the back (spine). A knife 30 mm tall and 5 mm thick gives $MA \approx 6$: each 200 N of baton force pushes the wood apart with about 1,200 N. Friction takes a large share in practice, which is why slick, well-ground blades and dry, straight grain split best.

- Straight-grained wood splits along planes where fibers separate easily: little force is needed.
- At a **knot**, fibers wrap around the branch and run across the split. The wedge must break fibers instead of separating them, the force needed rises many-fold, and the blade jams or twists.

### Moisture and wood tools

Green wood (30–50 % moisture, wet basis; Stage 3) is soft to carve and tough. Dry wood (10–15 %) is harder and stiffer, but can be brittle. Wood also **shrinks** as it dries, and more across the grain than along it, which cracks tools that dry too fast. That is why you carve green and dry slowly (bag it with shavings), and why a digging-stick tip is dried near the coals but never charred. Charcoal has almost no strength.

## Examples

**Temperate forest, wet autumn:** baton wrist-thick dead-standing wood to reach dry cores, then make feather sticks (Stage 3). Carve pegs from forked hazel dead-fall.

**Boreal winter:** cold hands lose dexterity (Stage 8), so carve in short sessions, rewarm your hands, and use a larger handle or gloves with grip. Frozen wood splits easily but also shatters, so keep strikes controlled.

**Desert:** hard, dense woods (mesquite, acacia, olive) dull blades quickly. Saw them rather than baton them. A digging stick is useful for reaching damp sand under a dry wash (Stage 4).

**Tropical:** a machete does the knife's heavy work. The same blood-circle rules apply with a longer radius. Bamboo edges are razor-sharp, so handle split bamboo with gloves.

**Coastal:** salt corrodes carbon steel overnight. Dry and oil the blade daily. Driftwood often hides stones and nails, so inspect it before batoning.

**Urban/disaster:** a multitool's saw and pliers do more than a knife. Improvise pry bars (levers) and wedges from timber, but never baton with a kitchen or folding knife.

## Common mistakes

- Carving towards yourself or over your lap, with the femoral artery in the blade’s path.
- Batoning with a folding knife, or through a knot.
- Letting someone walk into the blood circle, or carving while talking to someone beside you.
- "A dull knife is safer." (Myth.) It needs much more force and skates unpredictably.
- "Fire-harden" a digging stick by charring the tip. (Myth as usually done.) Gentle drying hardens; charring leaves weak charcoal.
- Leaving the knife stuck in a log or the ground.
- Carving while cold, tired or hurried at dusk: exactly the Stage 1 human-factor traps.

## Practical exercises

### Knife-safety drill and a carved tent peg

> [!WARNING]
> **Supervised.** Only with a competent person present.
>
> The first sessions need a competent adult supervisor (always for minors). Seated, elbows on knees, working outside the knee, blood circle clear, first-aid kit open and within reach.

Level 3 (Safe physical) · 👥 Supervised · about 60 min

**Materials:** Fixed-blade or locking knife, sharp; Straight dead-fall stick or a 20 mm dowel; Leather or thick fabric pad; First-aid kit with a pressure dressing

**Steps**

1. Mark your blood circle; have your partner step in and practise stopping immediately.
2. Practise handing over the knife handle first, saying "thank you", and sheathing it.
3. Carve a 25 cm tent peg: point one end with cuts away from your body, then cut a notch 4 cm from the top (two stop-cuts, then pare out between them).
4. Stop when you are cold or tired. Reflect: where did the blade go when it slipped?

**You have it when**

- No cut ever pointed at a body part.
- The peg has a clean point and a notch that holds a cord under tension.
- Your partner confirms the circle and passing were always respected.

Builds the skill: Safe knife work and carving.

### Baton-split and make feather sticks

> [!WARNING]
> **Supervised.** Only with a competent person present.
>
> Supervised. Never a folding knife. Kneel to the side of the block; the free hand stays clear. Stop at any knot.

Level 3 (Safe physical) · 👥 Supervised · about 45 min

**Materials:** Full-tang fixed-blade knife; Wooden baton; Wrist-thick, knot-free dead-standing rounds; Chopping block; Safety glasses

**Steps**

1. Choose rounds: straight grain, no knots, 20 cm long, wrist-thick or smaller.
2. Set the blade across the end grain with 5+ cm of tip protruding. Strike the spine, then the tip, keeping the blade level.
3. Split to quarters, then carve a feather stick from one quarter.
4. Try one knotty piece with a saw and wedge instead, and compare the effort.

**You have it when**

- Clean splits with the blade kept level.
- At least one usable feather stick.
- You can explain the wedge and why knots stop it.

Builds the skill: Fire preparation.

## Scenario question

Subarctic forest, −8 °C, 16:20, light failing at 16:50. Your partner is shivering slightly and trying to baton a knotty, thigh-thick spruce log with a folding knife to get dry wood for the evening fire. Her fingers are clumsy and she is hurrying. You have a folding saw in your pack and there are dead lower spruce branches all around.

**What do you do?**

1. Hold the log steady for her, with your hand close to the blade so it cannot twist.
2. Stop her, add a layer, and switch to dead branches and the saw; split only knot-free wood.
3. Let her keep going since she is nearly through, and tell her to slow down a little.
4. Take over and baton the knotty log yourself with the same folding knife, more carefully.

<details>
<summary>Best choice and debrief</summary>

**Best: 2.** Several risk factors stack here: wrong tool, wrong wood, cold hands, time pressure. Remove as many as you can: better fuel (dead branches break by hand), the right tool (saw), and warm hands (Stage 1 heat balance, Stage 8 cold physiology). A cut at −8 °C and dusk would also bring bleeding control, more heat loss and a possible evacuation, so preventing it is by far the cheapest option.

- **1.** Puts your hand in the blood circle and in the blade path, with a knife that will likely fold or jam.
- **2.** Best: it removes the hazard, addresses the cold that is degrading her dexterity, and gets fuel faster.
- **3.** Folding knife, knot, cold hands and hurry together are the classic setup for a serious cut far from help.
- **4.** Same hazard, same knife: the knot and the folding blade are the problem.

</details>

## Summary

- Blood circle: no one within arm plus blade; stop cutting if someone enters.
- Cut away from yourself, work outside the knee (femoral artery), and keep the support hand behind the edge.
- Sharp is safer: pressure = force ÷ area, so a keen edge needs a small, controllable push.
- Baton only with a full-tang fixed blade, through knot-free wrist-thick wood. The knife is a wedge (MA ≈ h/t).
- Wooden tools: carve green, dry slowly, harden by drying (not charring). Carry a first-aid kit and take a WFA course.
