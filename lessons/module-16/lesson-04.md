# 16.4 · Flood, wildfire and extreme weather at home

Weather disasters usually arrive with warning. The people who die are disproportionately those who waited too long, drove into floodwater, stayed alone in a hot flat, or heated a cold one with a fuel-burning device. Clear triggers and a few household routines turn warning time into safety.

## Explanation

For most weather hazards you get **warning** — hours for a flood or a wildfire, days for a cyclone or a heatwave. What kills is not the lack of warning but **acting too late**: people wait to see how bad it gets, and then the road is flooded, the smoke is thick, or the power is already off. This lesson is about deciding in advance.

### Shelter in place or evacuate?

The question is simple: **will the place you are in be overrun, or is the outside the danger?**

- If **your location will be overrun** — rising water, an approaching fire front, a tsunami zone, a damaged building — **leave early**.
- If **the outside air or sky is the danger** — a chemical release, thick smoke, a tornado, a blizzard, a hurricane’s winds when you are outside the surge zone — **shelter in place**.
- If authorities **order an evacuation of your zone, go**. Evacuation orders are based on information you do not have (river forecasts, fire spread models, dam status).

![Decision flow: if an official evacuation order applies, go. If the hazard comes to you and outside air is dangerous (chemical plume, tornado, smoke), shelter in place. If your location will be overrun (flood, wildfire, tsunami zone, damaged building), leave early by a planned route.](../../assets/diagrams/s16-evac-decision.svg)

*A simple decision flow. Reassess at every new alert; leaving early is almost never wrong.*

### Personal evacuation triggers

Stage 12 taught **go/no-go triggers** for routes. The home version is a short list, written before the season, of observations that mean **“we leave now”** — no discussion. For example:

- any official evacuation **warning** (not only the order) while you have children, older people, pets or livestock to move;
- water reaching a marked point on the street, or the river gauge passing a set level;
- smoke or embers visible, or a fire reported within a set distance on a high fire-danger day;
- the last safe route out about to close (a low bridge, a ford, the only forest road).

Pre-commitment beats in-the-moment judgment for the same reason turnaround times do: under stress, “let’s wait a bit longer” always sounds reasonable.

### Sheltering in place

- **Chemical release / heavy smoke:** get inside, close and lock windows and doors, turn off ventilation and air-conditioning that draws outside air, go to an interior room (above ground for gases heavier than air), seal gaps with plastic sheeting and tape if told, and listen to the radio.
- **Tornado / violent wind:** lowest floor, interior room without windows (a bathroom or closet), protect your head; mobile homes and cars are not shelters.
- **Cyclone/hurricane outside the surge zone:** interior room, away from windows; stay inside during the calm eye.

### Floods

Floods are among the most frequent and deadliest disasters worldwide, and most flood deaths happen to people **walking or driving into moving water**. Stage 12 explained the physics; the home rules are:

- **Turn around, don’t drown.** About **15 cm (6 in) of moving water can knock an adult over; about 30 cm (1 ft) can sweep away most cars**, and 60 cm (2 ft) moves SUVs and trucks. You cannot see the road, open drains or washed-out sections under floodwater.
- **Evacuate early** if you are in a flood zone and told to go; move people, pets, medicines and documents first, then valuables upstairs.
- **If trapped by rising water:** go to the highest floor — **not a closed attic**, where rising water can trap you; go onto the roof only if necessary, and signal for help.
- **Switch off electricity** at the main switch if you can do it standing on a dry floor; never touch electrical equipment while wet or standing in water.
- **After:** floodwater carries sewage, chemicals and debris. Assume your well and anything it touched are contaminated; wear boots and gloves; watch for downed lines, gas leaks, structural damage and CO from generators and pumps used in clean-up.

**Flash floods** (Stage 12) can come from storms you cannot see; in canyons, washes and urban underpasses, move to higher ground at the first warning.

### Wildfire

Most homes lost in wildfires are ignited by **embers** and small flames — which can travel a kilometre or more ahead of the fire front — not by a wall of flame. So preparation is about the few metres around the house (the **home ignition zone**) and about **leaving early**.

![Home ignition zone: 0 to 1.5 metres non-combustible, no mulch or stored wood; 1.5 to 9 metres lean, clean and green, spaced plants, trimmed trees; 9 to 30 metres reduced fuel; embers travel far ahead of the fire front and ignite most homes](../../assets/diagrams/s16-ignition-zone.svg)

*The home ignition zone: the closer to the house, the more strictly fuel is removed.*

- **Before the season:** clean gutters and roofs, remove anything burnable within 1.5 m of the walls (mulch, woodpiles, doormats), screen vents, trim branches, keep a hose and ladder ready, and know two routes out.
- **Ready, Set, Go:** be **ready** (kit, plan), stay **set** (monitor alerts on high fire-danger days, go-bags in the car, pets close), and **go** early — leaving late in thick smoke on a narrow road is one of the deadliest situations in wildfire.
- **If caught by fire while driving:** do not drive into smoke you cannot see through; park away from vegetation, keep the engine running, close windows and vents, get below window level under a wool blanket, and call for help. A car or a solid house protects far better than being outside.
- **If trapped at home:** stay inside, away from outside walls, close doors and windows, fill sinks and bathtubs, and check for spot fires once the front passes.
- **Smoke:** even far from the fire, smoke harms people with asthma, heart disease, older people, children and pregnant women. Stay indoors with windows closed, use a HEPA air cleaner if you have one, and consider a clean-air shelter.

### Extreme heat

Heatwaves kill more people in many countries than any other weather hazard — quietly, often older people alone in hot flats. At home:

- Keep heat **out by day** (close windows, curtains and shutters on sunny sides), **flush it out at night** (open windows for cross-ventilation when outside air is cooler), live in the coolest room, use cool showers, wet cloths and foot baths.
- Drink regularly; eat light meals; avoid exertion in the hottest hours.
- **Fans** help up to a point, but in very high heat (above roughly 35 °C) fans alone do not prevent heat illness — use an air-conditioned place or a **cooling centre**.
- **Check on older neighbours and anyone living alone twice a day.** Know the signs of heat stroke (confusion, hot skin, collapse) — it is a medical emergency (Stage 8).

### Extreme cold and winter storms

- Keep one room warm: interior, small, doors closed, windows covered with blankets or plastic, everyone together, layers, hats, sleeping bags.
- **Never** heat with a gas oven, barbecue, camping stove or generator indoors — carbon monoxide kills more people in winter storms than many realise.
- Prevent frozen pipes (let a tap drip if water still runs; know how to shut off and drain the system if you leave).
- If the house is becoming dangerously cold, go to friends or a **warming centre early** — especially with infants, older people or anyone ill.

### Temporary shelter

In order of preference: **friends or relatives** outside the affected area, **hotels**, then **public shelters** (evacuation centres, warming or cooling centres). Take your go-bag, medications, bedding, chargers, ID, and food and supplies for pets (most public shelters accept only service animals — plan a pet-friendly option). **Register** at the shelter so family and authorities can find you. Sleeping in a car is a last resort — never with the engine running in a closed or snow-covered space.

## Scientific and technical background

### An evacuation time budget

Whether you can still leave safely is a time budget, like the daylight budget in Stage 1:

$$
\text{margin} = t_{\text{hazard}} - (t_{\text{decide}} + t_{\text{prepare}} + t_{\text{travel}})
$$

where $t_{\text{hazard}}$ is the time until the hazard closes your route. Worked example: a fire is reported 12 km away, spreading towards you at about 3 km/h — about **4 h** before it arrives, but embers and smoke will close the single forest road perhaps an hour earlier: say 3 h. You need 20 min to decide, 40 min to load people, pets and documents, and traffic will make a normally 30-minute drive take 90 minutes: $3\text{ h} - (0.33 + 0.67 + 1.5)\text{ h} = 0.5$ h. A 30-minute margin on an uncertain fire is thin: this is a **go now** — and every minute spent deciding or packing valuables comes straight out of the margin.

### Why shallow moving water is so dangerous

The drag force on a body in a current grows with the **square** of the speed ($F \propto \rho v^2 A$, Stage 12): water at 3 m/s pushes about **nine times** as hard as water at 1 m/s. A car also **floats**: a typical car displaces enough water to become buoyant in about 30–60 cm, losing tyre grip completely.

## Examples

**River valley town (flood).** The family’s trigger is the council’s evacuation warning or water over the lowest street corner. They move the car to high ground the evening before and leave by the upstream bridge before it closes.

**Forest-edge suburb (wildfire).** On extreme fire-danger days the go-bags are in the car, the pets are indoors, and the plan is to leave at the first warning for the area — not to wait for flames.

**Hot desert city (heatwave, power cut).** The older couple next door have no air-conditioning; the neighbours check them morning and evening and drive them to a cooling centre on the hottest afternoon.

**Tropical cyclone coast.** The house is in a storm-surge zone: the family evacuates inland to relatives when told, rather than “riding it out”; a neighbour outside the surge zone shelters in an interior bathroom.

**Subarctic village (blizzard, outage).** The family closes off all but one room, uses sleeping bags, and runs the generator only outside the house, far from windows; they go to the community hall when the indoor temperature falls below 10 °C.

**Industrial town (chemical release).** Sirens and phone alerts say *shelter in place*: windows shut, ventilation off, interior room, radio on — driving through the plume would be worse.

## Common mistakes

- Waiting to “see how bad it gets” before evacuating.
- Walking or driving through floodwater — even water that looks shallow.
- Sheltering in a closed attic during a flood.
- Myth: “Fans keep you safe in any heat.” In very high heat they do not prevent heat illness.
- Myth: “Taping an X on windows protects them in a storm.” It does not stop glass breaking and can make larger, more dangerous shards; use shutters or boards, or stay away from windows.
- Heating a cold home with a gas oven, barbecue or generator indoors.
- Leaving pets behind because the plan never included them.
- Returning home before authorities say it is safe.

## Practical exercises

### Write your evacuation triggers

Level 2 (Simulation) · 🏠 Home · about 40 min

**Steps**

1. For each of your top hazards (from Lesson 1), write the observations or alerts that mean “we leave now”.
2. Mark on a map your two routes out and the point where each would be cut (low bridge, ford, forest road).
3. Work out an evacuation time budget: decide + prepare + travel. How much warning do you need?
4. Agree the triggers with everyone in the household and post them with the plan.

**You have it when**

- Each hazard has at least one observable trigger.
- You know how long you need to leave, with a margin.

Builds the skill: Household emergency plan and kit.

### Prepare a room to shelter in place

Level 3 (Safe physical) · 🏠 Home · about 45 min

**Materials:** Plastic sheeting; Duct tape; Scissors; Radio

**Steps**

1. Choose an interior room with few windows and access to water and a toilet if possible.
2. Measure and pre-cut plastic sheeting for its windows, vents and door; label and store it with tape in the room.
3. Find how to switch off your ventilation or air-conditioning.
4. Time how long it takes to get everyone in and the room sealed (practise with the sheets held in place, not taped to painted surfaces).

**You have it when**

- Materials are cut, labelled and stored in the room.
- Everyone knows how to switch off ventilation.

Builds the skill: Household emergency plan and kit.

## Scenario question

Late afternoon, extreme fire-danger day. You live on a forest-edge street with one road out through the trees. Your area has just received an evacuation **warning** (not yet an order). You can see a smoke column about 10 km away; the wind is towards you. At home: your partner, a baby, a dog. Your partner wants to clear the gutters and wet the roof first.

**What do you do?**

1. Clear gutters and wet the roof, then leave if an order comes.
2. Leave now with the baby, dog, go-bags and medications by the planned route; tell the out-of-area contact; register where you go.
3. Wait for the order; warnings are often lifted.
4. Stay and shelter in the house — it is solid brick.

<details>
<summary>Best choice and debrief</summary>

**Best: 2.** This is Stage 12’s go/no-go logic and Stage 1’s turnaround time applied to home: triggers set in calm conditions protect you from the “just a bit longer” trap. Wildfire deaths cluster among late evacuees on smoke-filled roads. Property preparation belongs before the season; on the day, people leave early.

- **1.** That work should have been done before the season; now it spends the margin on your only road out.
- **2.** Best: your pre-set trigger (a warning, with a baby and one road out) has been met. Leaving early is the single most protective wildfire action.
- **3.** Orders are sometimes issued when roads are already smoky and jammed; with a baby and one road, the margin is too thin.
- **4.** Sheltering in a house is a last resort when trapped, not a plan — especially with a baby.

</details>

## Summary

- **Overrun → leave early; outside air/sky is the danger → shelter in place; ordered → go.**
- Write **personal evacuation triggers** before the season, and follow them without debate.
- Floods: **turn around, don’t drown** — 15 cm of moving water knocks you down, 30 cm moves cars; no closed attics.
- Wildfire: clear the **home ignition zone**; **Ready, Set, Go**; embers ignite most homes.
- Heat and cold: manage one room, **never fuel-burning heat indoors**, use cooling/warming centres, **check on vulnerable neighbours**.
- Temporary shelter: friends first, then public shelters — bring kit, medicines, pet plan, and register.
