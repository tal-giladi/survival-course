# 19.2 · Desert survival

Hot-environment emergencies move fast: a person walking in the midday desert can lose 1–1.5 L of sweat an hour, and heat stroke can develop within an afternoon. The decisions that prevent it — shade, rest, water discipline, staying findable — are simple, but they run against every instinct to act.

## Explanation

### Briefing

A radiator hose splits at 11:00 on a remote desert track. It is 41 °C and rising to 44 °C. You and a friend have **5 L of water**, a sunshade, a tool roll, a signal mirror and a trip plan lodged with someone in town. The highway is 38 km away.

Desert emergencies punish the instinct to *do something*. Every litre of sweat is a litre you cannot get back, and the heat illness that follows destroys judgment right when you need it. The skilled response looks almost passive: stop the heat gain, rest in real shade, drink what your body needs, make the vehicle visible, and wait for the evening.

> [!CAUTION]
> **Heat stroke is a time-critical emergency**
>
> Any confusion, odd behaviour, collapse or seizure in a hot person is heat stroke until proven otherwise — **even if they are still sweating**. Cool first and aggressively (cold-water immersion if available; otherwise soak the whole body and fan continuously), then evacuate. Do not give paracetamol or aspirin for it. Learn this hands-on on a wilderness first-aid course.

## Examples

**Debrief themes**

- **Ration sweat, not water.** Water in the jerrycan does nothing for you; water in your body keeps you cooling and thinking.
- **The vehicle is the signal.** From the air a car is visible from kilometres away; two people on foot are nearly invisible.
- **Night is for movement — only if movement is necessary.** If nobody knows where you are and you know exactly where help is, travel in the cool of the evening and night. If a trip plan names your route, stay.
- **The same logic elsewhere:** a canyon hike in summer (shade, turn around, wait for evening), an outback breakdown, a heatwave power cut in a city (cool rooms, check on vulnerable people, water on hand).

## Common mistakes

- Rationing drinking water below need to “make it last” (a myth).
- Working on the vehicle or walking in the hottest part of the day.
- Sheltering inside a parked car, which can exceed 60 °C.
- Assuming someone who is still sweating cannot have heat stroke.
- Treating heat stroke with fever medicine instead of immediate cooling.
- Leaving the vehicle when someone knows your route — or walking out at night when help is already on its way.

### Vehicle heat-emergency audit and plan

> [!WARNING]
> **Home.** Safe to do at home or at a desk.
>
> Never sit in a closed car on a hot day to “test” it, and never leave people or animals inside.

Level 4 (Integrated) · 🏠 Home · about 90 min

**Materials:** Your vehicle; Pen and paper or a spreadsheet; A thermometer (optional)

**Steps**

1. Inventory what your vehicle carries today for a 24-hour breakdown in heat: water, shade material, signals, repair tape, first aid, lights.
2. Calculate water for everyone who usually travels with you: rest-in-shade and working-in-sun rates for 24 hours.
3. Write a one-page trip plan template: route, vehicle description, check-in time, and the time your contact should raise the alarm.
4. Optional: on a hot day, measure the temperature inside the closed car and in open shade next to it, from a safe position — never by staying inside.

**You have it when**

- A written kit list with gaps identified and fixed.
- A trip-plan template you have used at least once.

Builds the skill: Vehicle emergency readiness.

## Scenario question

A summer canyon hike. At 12:30 your partner, who has been drinking little, becomes confused and starts slurring words; they are hot to the touch and still sweating. It is 39 °C. You have 2 L of water, a pool of shaded creek water nearby, and one bar of signal on the rim 20 minutes above.

**What do you do first?**

1. Give them paracetamol and a litre to drink, then rest in the shade.
2. Get them into the shaded creek water up to the neck (supporting their head) or, if that is unsafe, soak and fan them continuously; then send for help once cooling is underway.
3. Leave them and climb to the rim to call for help immediately.
4. Help them walk out to the trailhead before they get worse.

<details>
<summary>Best choice and debrief</summary>

**Best: 2.** Altered mental status in the heat is **heat stroke**. Current WMS and ACSM guidance: **cool first, transport second**. Immersion in cool water, with someone holding the casualty’s head clear, is the gold standard; otherwise whole-body wetting and continuous fanning. Only then go for the call — or send someone.

- **1.** Antipyretics don’t treat heat stroke, and a confused person may not swallow safely.
- **2.** Best — cold-water immersion is the most effective cooling; cool first, then call.
- **3.** The call matters, but minutes of cooling matter more; leaving a confused person alone is also dangerous.
- **4.** Exertion in the heat is what caused this; walking will worsen it.

</details>

## Summary

- Ration sweat, not water: shade, rest, timing.
- Raised double-layer shade beats a parked car.
- Confusion in the heat = heat stroke: cool first, transport second.
- Stay with the vehicle when someone knows your route; travel only in the cool, and only if you must.
- Prepare signals early; fire them when someone can see them.
